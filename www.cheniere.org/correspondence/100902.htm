Hence they become sensitive to initial conditions.  Many such systems exhibit peculiar phenomena as a result: you can sometimes turn it off, and the next time it starts it will behave differently with different COP.  By starting and stopping it several times, one can adjust back to a more usual "initial condition" so as to resume performance.  There is also much other odd phenomenology involved that does not appear in the textbooks.  There is, e.g., a decay mechanism that nature uses for such "excited" far from equilibrium states.  We have included much of this new phenomenology and the "decay mechanism" (and how to solve it) in our cited book, Energy from the Vacuum, 2002. The trick the researcher has to learn how to do is to establish what is known in advanced thermodynamics as a stable disequilibrium state.  See discussions in Kondepudi and Prigogine, Modern Thermodynamics, Wiley, 1998 (corrected edition 1999).  Also see p. 459 therein for a summary of several known areas that seriously violate thermodynamics theory, and are recognized to do so.
