The Law and Personal Firearms
by
Rick Berry

In this essay, I’m concerned with the relevant federal laws and rulings. Whatever your personal beliefs regarding externally imposed laws regarding defensive arms, you should be aware of all state and local laws which might apply in your area. It is far easier to protect yourself against state-sanctioned attack if you understand what you are guarding against. Further, I recommend that you study the relevant federal laws yourself; they are in plain English and are not too difficult to understand. You should be aware that I am not a lawyer, nor am I a trained gunsmith, nor a machinist. Those caveats aside, I am providing this information as carefully and completely as I know how. You are responsible for your own actions, and for the consequences of those actions. Firearms are deadly tools, doubly so if not respected or understood. Don’t come crying to me if you shoot your finger off, or put your eye out, or kill your neighbor through ignorance or folly.

Now, on to the good stuff…. I begin with a bold assertion:

It is legal to build your own firearms!

That might come as a shock to many, but it’s true. As long as a person is not prohibited from buying and possessing a firearm in the normal way, he is also not prohibited from building that type of firearm for himself.

I say “not prohibited” because the relevant laws discuss what is prohibited and illegal; they do not confer rights or state what is “legal”. Remember, what is not prohibited by law, is legal.

The relevant laws and regulations require licenses and tax payments in order to be “engaged in the business” of manufacturing, importing or dealing in firearms. Further taxes and licensing are required for making “NFA” firearms (National Firearms Act Title 2 firearms: machine guns, destructive devices, silencers, short barreled rifles, etc). 1

These laws and regulations requiring licenses do not apply to people who are not “engaged in the business.” 2

What this means for you is that you can make your own legal “Personal Firearms” without any special license or approval from the federal government.

Here’s a quote from the ATF “Frequently Asked Questions” website that gets to the heart of the matter:

    (A7) Does the GCA prohibit anyone from making a handgun, shotgun or rifle? With certain exceptions a firearm may be made by a nonlicensee provided it is not for sale and the maker is not prohibited from possessing firearms. However, a person is prohibited from making a semiautomatic assault weapon or assembling a nonsporting semiautomatic rifle or nonsporting shotgun from imported parts. In addition, the making of an NFA firearm requires a tax payment and approval by ATF. An application to make a machinegun will not be approved unless documentation is submitted showing that the firearm is being made for a federal or state agency. [18 U. S. C. 922( o), (r), (v), and 923, 27 CFR 178.39, 178.40, 178.41 and 179.105]
    http://www.atf.treas.gov/firearms/faq/faq2.htm#a7

The “GCA” is the Gun Control Act of 1968. 3. The “However” sentence says basically that the firearms you make have to comply with all the laws which apply to any other firearms… that is, you can only make legal firearms. “Semiautomatic Assault Weapons” and NFA weapons or devices are not legal in this context.

A nonlicensee cannot make a firearm “for sale.” What this means exactly is highly debatable. The BATFE has not commented on exactly when a person may sell a “personal firearm” without violating the law. Some people will quite forcefully project their opinions on the matter, stating them as fact. The truth is, we don’t know what the truth is. If you make firearms for your own use, you can almost certainly will them to your heirs. You might be able to sell a personal firearm or two from your collection, in the case of financial hardship. Selling ten “personal firearms” per month is almost certainly going to be considered “manufacturing” and is likely to come to the attention of the BATFE. Somewhere in between is the real answer, but until someone causes a ruling to be made, pro or con, we don’t know where the line is. Some people claim there’s a limit of making one PF per year, some say one per lifetime. I can find no basis for these opinions. Perhaps they come from ill-informed opinions of FFL Licensees or from ATF agents. In any case, discretion is probably the safest course for staying out of trouble.

So what can you make? You can make a rifle, a shotgun or a handgun. Again, these have to be in legal configurations, and you have to be of the required age to purchase the type of firearm in question (e.g. over 21 to make a handgun). Restrictions on the legal configurations for rifles, shotguns and pistols can get pretty complex, especially when it comes to semiautomatic weapons, but with a little attention to the texts, it is quite possible to avoid trouble. Rifles have a rifled barrel of 16 inches or more and the overall length of a rifle must be 26 inches or more. A “Short Barreled Rifle” is an NFA Title 2 firearm. Shotguns have a smooth bore and a barrel of 18 inches or more, and the overall length of a shotgun must be 26 inches or more. A “Short Barreled Shotgun” is an NFA Title 2 firearm. Handguns are firearms designed to be held and fired with one hand. A smoothbore handgun is an NFA “Any Other Weapon” Title 2 firearm.

The “Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994” 4 contained a revision to the Gun Control Act to add the definition of, and restriction against the manufacture, transfer or possession of, certain “semiautomatic assault weapons.” This revision is known as the Assault Weapons Ban (AWB). It also added restrictions on the capacity of magazines, limiting new magazines to 10 rounds. While this law may ‘sunset’ or automatically die in September of 2004, it is important to understand the definition of “semiautomatic assault weapon.” 5

A “semiautomatic assault rifle” has an ability to accept a detachable magazine and has at least 2 of— a folding or telescoping stock; a pistol grip that protrudes conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon, a bayonet mount, a flash suppressor or threaded barrel designed to accommodate a flash suppressor, and a grenade launcher.

A “semiautomatic assault shotgun” has at least 2 of-- a folding or telescoping stock, a pistol grip that protrudes conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon, a fixed magazine capacity in excess of 5 rounds, and an ability to accept a detachable magazine.

A “semiautomatic assault pistol” has an ability to accept a detachable magazine and has at least 2 of-- an ammunition magazine that attaches to the pistol outside of the pistol grip, a threaded barrel capable of accepting a barrel extender, flash suppressor, forward handgrip, or silencer, a shroud that is attached to, or partially or completely encircles, the barrel and that permits the to hold the firearm with the nontrigger hand without being burned, a manufactured weight of 50 ounces or more when the pistol is unloaded, and is a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm. 6

So, to be legal, a semiautomatic rifle with a detachable magazine can only have one of the “evil features” from the list. If the rifle has both a detachable magazine and a pistol grip, no further evil features are permissible. If the magazine is fixed in the weapon, it no longer qualifies for “semiautomatic assault weapon” status. Likewise, with a pistol, only one “evil feature” besides a detachable magazine is permissible, if the pistol is to remain legal.

Firearms which existed before the AWB took effect are exempted from the restrictions, (i.e. “grandfathered”) and are known as “Preban” firearms. Their prices have risen because of their status, and presumably will fall again if and when the ban sunsets. Any new firearms are by definition “Post ban” and must comply with the restrictions of the GCA (including the AWB unless it sunsets) and the NFA.

Federal law considers the receiver or frame of a firearm to be the regulated part. In other words, the receiver IS the gun. All the rest of the firearm is “just parts.” A person who buys a whole firearm or just the receiver of a firearm from a licensed dealer (an “FFL dealer”, or “Federal Firearms License holder”, in other words, from a gunstore) fills out Form 4473, “Firearms Transaction Record,” also known as the “yellow form”. The gunstore or dealer keeps this form.

A person who creates his own receiver need not file any federal paperwork, nor engrave any particular markings or serial numbers on the receiver (though, as always, local laws may require registration, or prohibit making firearms). It is a good idea to engrave some kind of serial number, for purposes of identifying your property if it should be stolen then recovered, or somehow be confiscated by law enforcement for whatever reason.

A person who buys all the other parts besides the receiver required to assemble a firearm doesn’t have to fill out any paperwork. These parts can be bought directly from suppliers and distributors, and shipped via USPS or common carriers with no restriction. Folks have been doing this for a long time with AR15 type weapons, buying the bare “stripped receiver” through an FFL dealer, then shopping for the parts. One reason for this is to sidestep the federal $200.00 excise tax on “manufacturing” a long gun. There is no provision for nonlicensees to pay this tax, so it can not be collected.

A person who makes his own receiver just doesn’t have to go through the process of transferring the controlled part through an FFL dealer, and filling Form 4473, since he is himself the maker, and no transfer is occurring. This is appealing to different people for different reasons.

Glossary

    Title 1/Title 2/Class 3 firearms
  • Licensee: A person who is “engaged in the business” and licensed by the BATFE to do something, e.g. manufacture, import or deal in firearms.
  • Nonlicensee: A person who is not “engaged in the business,” and thus not regulated by the laws which apply to those “engaged in the business” and not required therefore to be Licensed. In other words, a regular person.
  • Manufacture: What someone “engaged in the business” does when creating a firearm; intended for sale.
  • Make: What a nonlicensee does when creating a firearm; not for sale.
  • “Assemble”: To put together parts and a receiver which form a whole firearm. Not Make, nor Manufacture.
