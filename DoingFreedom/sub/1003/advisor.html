Food and Freedom

Dear Advisor,

I've been seeing alot of news stories about 'food cops' and laws being passed against junk food in schools and stuff. Lots of people seem to think this is a problem, that we're not 'free' with these laws. With so many people being fat and all, and my stolen money paying for their healthcare, why shouldn't their [sic] be laws like this? What's wrong with suing McDonald's for their junk food?

How is all this hurting my freedom?

Healthy Eater

Dear HE,

So many things to say in reply, and so little space! We'll try to cover all the bases.

First, do you believe everything you see/read on CNN? Much of the "obesity epidemic" is made-up nonsense, driven largely by the state. Back in 1998, the U.S. gov retooled its weight standards, which pushed some 30 million Americans into the "overweight" category who probably don't belong there. The Center for Consumer Freedom has an excellent article on this, and related issues. In fact, just going there and poking around ought to be a real eye-opener for you, and anyone else who buys into this obesity epidemic blather. Here's a short list of some actors now deemed overweight or even obese by the Thought Police standards: Keanu Reeves; Arnold Schwarzenegger; Harrison Ford; George Clooney; and Brad Pitt. Being fat never looked so good!

Second, how could any law that takes away an individual's freedom to choose not be an encroachment on individual liberty? While it's undoubtedly true that many individuals make poor dietary choices, it just doesn't follow from that that everyone's choices must be restricted. This kind of thinking -- we'll help control those who can't control themselves -- is responsible for a lot of the increase in the state's power over us. Motorcycle helmet laws, bike headgear laws, anti-skateboarding laws, prescription medication laws -- all of these and millions more laws interfere with our personal and economic freedoms. They try to shield people from the consequences of their choices, and inevitably lead to more and more irresponsible behavior. There's a lot of research that indicates, by the way, that sodas and other so-called junk foods aren't as harmful as some of the louder food nannies have made them out to be. CCF is an excellent resource for these. TechCentralStation also had a lengthy series on obesity that challenged several sacred cows; it's worth checking out too. Articles are best accessed from the author's archive.

It is indeed appalling and frustrating that tax money pays to enable people's poor choices. Supporting more laws that furthers that just adds to the problem, however. It feeds the entire tangled beast. That beast is composed of both the state and private businesses, and trying to kill it is one huge task. It should be obvious that state intervention of all sorts into health care should end. Prices would drop, taxes would decrease, and the result would be that more people could afford the health care they need.

In our view, private insurance feeds into this problem as well. It is a scheme that is designed to generate profits for the companies, rather than actually serving the individual policy-holder. Now, we don't have anything against profits -- it's the way in which these companies work that we don't like. Private insurance tries to protect individuals from the consequences of making poor choices (poor health/dietary choices and/or not saving for emergencies), and their administrative and bureaucratic costs add to the total cost of health care. There's nothing wrong with a straight fee-for-service approach to medical care -- which is how things used to be before the advent of "insurance". Think about it this way: the argument for insurance is that it helps individuals fulfill an essential need. Would you want a similar system to today's health care in place to help you meet other essential needs -- say, buying food or "allowing" access to oxygen?

On the matter of fast-food lawsuits -- as well as the other junk food lawsuits that seem to be starting up, sadly -- the issue comes down to individual choice and accepting responsibility for the consequences. Was the plaintiff force-fed Big Macs by the local McDonald's manager or staff? Were his or her protests of being full ignored, so that more calories were taken in than were needed? If so, then the complaint may be "actionable", but until then, these suits are simply more examples of individuals trying to duck responsibility for the consequences of their own poor choices. We're not big fans of fast food -- it costs more and is generally less healthy than a home-cooked meal -- but we do indulge occasionally and manage to do so without piling on pounds.

In sum, all this fast- and junk-food brouhaha is about using the machinery of the state -- laws and the courts -- against individuals in the guise of protecting them. Surely we don't need to spell it out more clearly than that.
