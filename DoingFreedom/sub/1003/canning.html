Canning and Preserving for Beginners
by
Rocky and Bullwinkle

So, you planted a garden for the first time, and got way more produce than you can use. The neighbors had their own gardens, so you couldn't even give the stuff away. What to do with all that delicious bounty?

Preserve it, of course! Why let your hard work in the garden go to waste simply because you can't consume it all fresh? Canning and preserving foods is an excellent way of making full use of all that you have, and most likely in a variety of ways.

Yes, canning can be labor-intensive. Yes, it's hot work, often on hot summer afternoons. But the jewel-colored jars gleaming from your pantry shelves in the winter will make it worth it, as will the exclamations should you decide to share some of your efforts as gifts. It doesn't take a lot of equipment or great skill to preserve foods. What it does require is time, care, and attention to detail.

The basics
Canning, dehydrating, smoking, or salting are all ways of preserving food. The idea is the same no matter the method -- treat the food so that bacteria and other microorganisms' growth is prohibited, or at least greatly retarded. Some techniques are generally thought of as being applied to only certain types of food -- meats are smoked, for example. But meats can be canned successfully, with proper care. They can also be dehydrated -- that's essentially how jerky is made. Some methods allow for more experimentation, or deviation from the recipe, than others. For example, drying meat to make jerky can involve any flavor combination you think good, as long as the sauce isn't too runny or applied too thickly. With canning, particularly pressure canning, acid levels of the food and the amount of processing time it takes to kill the microorganisms are closely related; adding extra acid (in the forms of cider, lemon juice, vinegar, tomato juice, or similar liquids) or leaving out acid ingredients can give you a big batch of botulism culture for all your efforts. Playing with those ingredients, or trying to shorten the processing time, can be disastrous and should not be attempted.

If you've images of pressure-cookers haunting your head at the thought of canning, relax. Many foods can be safely processed using a boiling water bath. The safest equipment to use is the trusty Ball or Kerr jars, with the two-part lids. The jar has to have a flat edge that's wide enough for the lid to form a good seal on -- don't use any old jar that looks cute, or like it could be a canning jar. The lid itself has a rubbery seal around the edge that, when processed and then cooled, gets sucked down against the flat edge of the canning jar and creates the seal. That process usually produces a satisfying "pop!" too -- a welcome sound after all your effort. The ring holds the lid in place during processing, and once the canned goods are cooled, can be removed to be washed and re-used (although it's good to keep them on if you're giving items as gifts, or if there are small children in the house). The lids need to be heated just to 120 F in water to be ready for use -- after being washed, of course. If they're overheated, the rubber edge loses its elasticity and may not form a proper seal. The rings, since they don't touch the food, don't need to be so carefully monitored, but as a safety precaution we always toss them in the hot water after they're washed as well.

All sorts of handy gadgets are available to make the canning process easier. Round funnels make the job of ladling food and liquid into jars much easier. Special tongs with a rubber-coated end fit easily and tightly around the necks of jars, making transporting your hot treasures much easier and safer. A magnet on a plastic stick fishes lids easily out of the hot water, and makes placing them on the hot jars a snap. Such "specialty" items may seem like extravagances, but their cost is minimal (and often they can be found very cheaply at estate sales and auctions) for the convenience and speed they bring to the canning process. A thermometer to monitor the temperature of the water the lids stay in until needed is a necessity, too -- any decent kitchen thermometer will be up to this task.

Clean water can be your friend, too. Who wants to go through all the effort of preparing fruit, cooking it, then canning it, only to see its beautiful glow dulled by hard water deposits left on the glass? Although it's highly recommended to wash jars in warm soapy water 12-24 hours after canning, that may not be enough to get the scale off without scrubbing. We've come to use only distilled water for all our processing, as it is absolutely nonreactive, and makes cleanup much easier. If you're canning solely for your own consumption, this may not be important, but if you plan to give treats as gifts, the extra cost of several gallons of water from Wal-Mart will be worth the time saved in cleaning.

Choose the food you use carefully. Use the highest quality items you can find, in order to get the best taste from your efforts. With fruits and vegetables, be especially careful to clean them well, and to remove bruised or discolored areas before cooking. For most recipes, fresh produce picked at the height of ripeness will work best. Some will yield acceptable results with slightly underripe produce -- some call for a mix of ripe and underripe produce -- and some will work well with frozen items. Read the recipe carefully before beginning, to ensure you have all ingredients and equipment on hand.

Starting simple: Dehydrating foods
This simple yet highly effective food preservation technique can be used on a wide variety of foods -- fruits and vegetables to meats and fish. If you wish, fairly compact and efficient food dehydrators can be purchased -- or, you can use your oven as long as it can maintain a steady temperature within a 5 F range. (Testing it in advance of your first batch of food is an excellent idea.) Oven dehydrating will be more expensive and less convenient than using a dehydrator; if your oven has hot or cool spots it may not be suitable for dehydrating food. Keep the foods roughly the same thickness and size, and place them in a single layer, allowing for good air circulation around each item.

Fruits are a natural for drying. They can be tasty snacks on their own, or can be combined with nuts and other items in your own trail mix blend. Fresh fruits work best, as frozen or canned usually have a higher water content. Slices, chunks, thin pieces dried fairly crisp or fat pieces that are chewy -- the choice is yours. To preserve vitamins (especially vitamin C, which is heat sensitive), dry them between 130-140 F. Many fruits should be pretreated, either with a weak lemon juice or ascorbic acid dip, to prevent browning or other discoloration during dehydration. If desired, you can combine fruit chunks with toppings -- say, a coconut, cinnamon, or sunflower sprinkle. These adhere best if the fruit is dipped in fruit juice or honey.

A fun twist on drying fruits is to make fruit leather. Kids love the expensive, preservative-filled fruit "rollups" that are available at any supermarket, but they'll love the homemade ones that they can customize to their own tastes even better. Preparing the fruit is simple: wash, seed/core/peel as necessary, and puree in a blender. If necessary, add liquid to get a thick but pourable consistency (again, your tastes will dictate the consistency you like best), and sweetener if desired or needed. If you wish, you can add spices -- cinnamon, nutmeg, and cloves are a few that would be very flavorful with many fruits. As you can cut away bruised or damaged spots, leathers are an excellent use for very ripe and damaged fruits.

Use a thick, food-grade plastic sheet (if you're using a dehydrator, it probably came with a tray suitable for this purpose, or you can buy them) and spray it lightly but thoroughly with vegetable spray. (Trust us: waxed paper does not cut it for this job!) Spread the fruit puree between 1/4 and 3/8-inch thick, and dry until it feels leather-like and there are no sticky spots. Working quickly, remove the leather from the tray while still warm, and cut and/or roll if desired. The fun flavors and blends you can create are endless -- apple pie, pumpkin pie, strawberry-rhubarb, cherry-almond, apple-pear, peach-raspberry -- the list goes on. This is an excellent way to get a nutritious food into picky or highly allergic kids without a fight -- and without a lot of added sugar or preservatives. It can be a very fun way for your kids to help in the kitchen, too, and test their culinary creativity.

Vegetables also stand up well to dehydration. While you may not think of many as being good for snacking, some -- sun-dried tomatoes -- are very popular and command high prices as gourmet items. Many veggies can be reconstituted and used as part of other recipes -- reconstituted carrots in place of grated carrots in a salad or coleslaw, for example. And of course, you can dry your own onions, garlic, shallots, and celery to make minced -- or powdered -- versions for use year-round. If you're really into preserving food, consider dehydrating chunks of a variety of vegetables (potatoes, peas, carrots, celery, and others) , blending them with spices, and sealing them -- your own camping food that just needs water and heat to eat and enjoy!

Canning: Preparation makes it easy
Many people think of canning as difficult, a remnant of our more hardy pioneer days. It's true that it is time intensive, but the rewards, especially in the cold of winter, make the initial investment well worth it. Some simple guidelines will help you turn out tasty canned goods with ease.

First, use only recipes that you know you can trust. Remember, you're dealing with fairly advanced food chemistry here -- you don't want to risk salmonella or botulism on yourself and your loved ones. Get a reputable canning cook book (pressure canners often have recipes in their manuals) and follow the recipes closely. Ball, of course, is still the top of the field, and their Blue Book of Canning offers more appealing ideas than you'll be able to try in one season. If you venture online for recipes, or want to try to develop one yourself, compare it to something as similar as you can in a good cookbook, and make sure the acid/salt proportions are comparable. Generally, adding spices -- say, cinnamon to an apple jelly recipe -- isn't going to affect the processing time nor the final product much. But substituting tomato juice for water in a recipe will affect it, and may render the product unsuitable for the canning technique given in the original recipe.

Have your processing containers and staging areas set up and clean in advance. We use a separate, small saucepan for heating lids and rings to 120 F, and a large canner or similar stockpot for processing the jars. The water needs to be deep enough to cover the jars, whether they're processed in single or double layers. Depending upon what you're canning you may need another large container of boiling water (e.g., to blanch vegetables or fruits). A high-powered camp stove or similar burner that can be used outside can be invaluable, for space as well as heat considerations. Wash the jars (preferably in a dishwasher) just before using. Have funnels, spoons, and all canning tools organized and cleaned before starting to process food. Also, have a designated place to set the hot jars when they come out of the water bath -- they'll need to sit for 12-24 hours before being washed, labeled, and stored. Anticipate the inevitable water drips and spills -- have paper towels handy, and rugs on the floor to help minimize the danger of falls.

Once all the tools and jars are ready, start preparing the food. Get the best-quality ingredients you can, at the height of freshness. There is some loss of flavor and/or texture with the canning process, so you don't want to start at a disadvantage. Clean them well, and trim bruised or damaged pieces. Meats should be trimmed of fat. Cut all fruits, vegetables, and/or meats into uniform sizes to ensure even cooking. Work quickly to clean and process the ingredients, to minimize discoloration and loss of nutritional content.

Cook the ingredients if necessary, following the recipe closely and adding any spices you may wish to make the product suit your taste. (You may wish to try recipes as-written in advance of doing this, so that you know what the finished product will taste like and can add spices more appropriately.) Pack them into hot, clean jars as directed, wipe the top clean, then place a lid and ring on. Hand-tighten and set aside until all jars are ready to be processed. Carefully add the jars to the boiling water, ensure that all jars are completely covered with water and are upright, and set the timer for the required processing time.

When the time is up, carefully remove the jars from the water bath, and set in the designated cooling place. Let dry and cool thoroughly -- a minimum of 12 hours. Wash jars well in warm sudsy water, then dry and label with both the name of the item and month and year it was canned. Store in a cool, dry, dark place (light may affect color and vitamin retention).

And that's it! Simple, easy canning that will provide you a wide variety of yummy treats, from tomato sauce to veggie spaghetti sauce, from apple jelly and applesauce to homemade canned pumpkin for completely home-made pies. For us, seeing the fruits of our garden turn into beautiful jars of preserves, sauces, salsas, and jellies is a reward unlike any other -- effortful, yes, but much more delicious than any store-bought jar could be.
