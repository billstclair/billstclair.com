Enemies Foreign and Domestic
A book review
by
Columba

What sort of spark would it take to ignite a second American Revolution, and what form might that Revolution take? These are the questions author Matthew Bracken has asked and answered in his new novel, Enemies Foreign and Domestic.

The story opens with a riveting account of the "Stadium Massacre," the root from which the plot grows. Firing from more than half a mile away, a sniper looses round after round into a packed football stadium. The shooter, an unbalanced Desert Storm veteran, is found and killed by a police marksman, but not before panic has broken out. In a terrified rush to get out of the stadium, thousands die. In the wake of the tragedy, Congress bans possession of all semi-auto rifles through the Schuleman-Montaine Firearms Safety Act - the Stamp Act of the 21st century. However, even at this early point, the official explanation of events fails to add up. The dead veteran's home is found full of expensive "assault rifles" and racist literature - things his friends and acquaintances find inconceivable. Regardless of what really happened at the stadium, gun owners are given a week to turn in all their semi-auto rifles or face years in prison. Some turn them in, many do not, and events begin to spiral out of control.

This is Mr. Bracken's first novel, and overall it is very good. My biggest complaint is that he focuses too exclusively on the Black Water group, and doesn't give much description of what is going on elsewhere in the country. I'd love to know how he thinks areas like the rural west would react to the events portrayed. The second half of the plot isn't as gripping as the first, and wider coverage of ongoing events could have alleviated this.

Still, I found it to be an engrossing read. The dramatic scenes sprinkled throughout its length are masterfully written. The hang glider and highway checkpoint scenes are just a sampling of the exquisitely crafted and thoroughly memorable points woven into the story. Also definitely worth mentioning is the pervasive realism in Enemies. Both the characters and plot of the novel are quite realistic. Mr. Bracken does a very good job keeping things practical and plausible. The protagonists aren't superheroes - they aren't perfect, and they don't always outwit or outshoot the bad guys.

And speaking of outshooting (didn't think I'd get through this without talking about guns, did you?), the scenes of violence are well though out as well as excellently written. When gun owners start shooting back, they do so with weapons commonly available to you and I (no 20mm cannon needed to resist these Feds). From old surplus bolt-action rifles to accurized target pistols to "borrowed" LEO submachineguns, Mr. Bracken paints what I believe to be a quite realistic picture of civilian resistance to out-of-control government agents. The detailed discussion of the guns is in-depth enough to keep an avid shooter happy, but stays relevant enough to not bore other readers (the amount of detail about marine gear does become tedious, though...).

All-in-all, Enemies is an enjoyable and highly relevant book and a very worthwhile addition to the freedom-lover's library. It's not perfect - there are areas of it that could use some improvement - but it's definitely worth purchasing and reading. Also, I hear there is a sequel in the works, to take place 5 years after the end of Enemies. I, for one, am definitely looking forward to it!

Mr. Bracken's web site includes ordering instructions and a substantial section of the book available for free (enough to make you REALLY want the rest...).
