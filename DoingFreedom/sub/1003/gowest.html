Go West, Young Man
by
Jac

The controller looked up from his computer screen as the booster neared the end of the track. As the explosive bolts fired and the stubby-winged shuttle lifted into the air, he adjusted his throat-mic and spoke; "Shuttle away, she's all yours, Orbital." Another day, another ounce, he thought as he turned back to his computer game and recommenced battling aliens for control of Aplha Centauri. As the bored controller blasted tentacled critters into random bits and bytes, the LEO shuttle Yellow Rose lifted toward orbit on a pillar of smoke and fire.

Jack Griffin looked up from his novel when he felt the Yellow Rose clamp against the transfer 'lock of the Low Earth Orbit Station Galileo. The captain's voice came over the 'com and asked the passengers to unbuckle their harnesses and disembark throught the foreward hatch. With a chuckle he added, "and thank you for riding the 'Devil's Roller Coaster'!"

Griffin, not caring much for lines and crowds, waited until all of the other passengers had left before exiting the hatch himself. Passing by the stewardess, he snapped his fingers as if he had just remembered something, and turned back towards her. "Why do they call it the 'Devil's Roller Coaster'?", he asked.

"Well," she replied with a grin, "when the Company first started building their tracked launch system, they discovered that the only people with practical experience building rails that could handle the high-g stresses of a rocket booster were roller-coaster engineers; so they hired a company that usually contracts out to amusement parks to build it." Griffin started laughing.

"Thanks," he said, then he checked his watch. "Damn, I'm gonna be late for my flight to Mars!" he exclaimed, "Bye!" Floating towards the access tunnel for his flight, Griffin saw with relief that he was not late after all. There were still quite a few people milling about outside the gate, many of them making last minute calls to their families Earthside. Having no family to call anyway, he passed them by so that he could check in while the ship was empty.

Griffin pushed himself into the reception room of the Interplanetary Liner Princess of Mars and looked around. Not bad, he thought to himself, not too shabby at all. Not exactly the QEII, but she was no tramp steamer.

He turned around as he felt a tap on his shoulder. "Excuse me, sir, may I take your bag and show you to your room?" A steward stood in front of him holding out his hand.

"Lead on, friend." The Princess of Mars wasn't a brand new ship, but she was fairly fresh out of dry dock where she had been refitted with a new, constant acceleration drive. Because of this, her decks had had to be reconfigured so that they sat crossways in the cylindrical hull. That way, when she reached a quarter-g acceleration, the passengers could have a floor beneath their feet, instead of a wall beneath their back. Griffin remembered the cross-sectional drawings he had seen of the Princess. She looks like a round skyscraper set on its side, he thought.

A few weeks later, Griffin had just finished his novel and was beginning to dig in his bag for another, when the 'com speaker on his wall beeped. A voice came on: All passengers and crew... please strap in for decceleration.

Half an hour later, the ship was again in free-fall. Griffin unstrapped himself from the room's acceleration couch and began gathering his things. As he did the realization of where he was finally hit him.

I can't believe it, he thought. I'm finally going to become a spacer!

He took his bag and made his way to the nearest hatch so he could get to a drop shuttle as soon as possible.

Griffin exited the Barsoom City spaceport and his senses were immediately assaulted on all sides. People who reffered to Mars as a frontier were right. The city looked, and sounded, like some strange composite of an Old West boom town, a carnival, and Disneyland. It didn't help that the giant dome overhead deflected all sound back down, either.

Shops and restaurants and hotels... even brothels, lined the main boulevard. Jack Griffin, seeing a sign advertising the "Barsoom Bar and Bordello," realized that he was going to have to seriously reconsider his beliefs about "normal" and "abnormal" businesses. Here, not much was considered abnormal and nothing was illegal.

As he made his way through the crush of people -- most of whom were wearing guns and/or a blade, he noticed -- he realized he had no idea where he was going.

"Excuse me sir, but I can't help but notice... you look a little lost."

Griffin turned and saw a young woman with a large automatic pistol riding low on her right hip and what looked like a short sword dangling from her left. Also, she seemed to be wearing some sort of uniform with a patch on her shirt. Her name tag read "Heather."

"Are you a police officer?" her asked.

She began to smirk, then tried to act a little more serious. "No, I'm not. Some of the other domes on Big Red have pi--, er, cops... but not B City!"

"Oh..."

"Actually, I work for LRT Security," she said. "We're contracted by the Main Street Business Association to provide security for the whole Strip, since it's basically a common area." She looked off to the side for a moment, trying to remember something. "Oh, right! Were you lost?"

"Um, yeah," replied Griffin, "I'm looking for the offices of Russell's Courier Service."

"Right," she said with a quick nod. "They're about two blocks back towards the port... right between Master Ling's Drug Emporium and Carl's Armory." Just then, a scuffle broke out a few yards to their left, and someone yelled something about "throwing down." Heather grimaced. "Oops," she said. "Looks like I'll have to go referee."

"Referee what?"

"The duel," she said, as she started walking towards the ruckus.

'The Tavern; was a relatively modest establishment by Mars standards. However, it had been there since the beginning, so it had plenty of room, and lots of patrons.

A man stumbled into the bar, obviously already drunk. His head hung low, and his eyes were barely visible slits. A few days growth of beard covered his chin. A heavy, hemp sand-robe covered his p-suited figure, but the sword at his side was clearly visible. Enlaid with gold on the sheath was a wolf's head, the logo of a paramilitary group from a close-by dome. This particular group provided "protection" to local merchants' caravans and Zeppelins.

The man walked up to the bar and ordered a drink.

"Why don't ya git yerself outta our bar, you damned pirate," a nearby man said loudly. From the half-empty bottle of cheap booze in his hand, and the blood-shot state of his eyes, the other patrons could tell that he, too, was on the wrong side of a buzz.

The bartender admonished the man. "Now, Robby, don't go making any trouble for yourself. This man hasn't done anything here."

Robby stood up. "Jes' 'cause he hasn't done nuthin' here don' mean he ain't done nuthin'."

The man at the bar took a swig from his drink, then turned to Robby. "You better do what your friend says... I came here for a drink, but I'd be happy to sharpen my blade on your bones."

Robby put his hand on his sword, "Well, throw down, then! Show us if your --heh, 'other parts'-- are as big as your mouth."

"Now, fellas," said the bartender, "I don't want nothin' happenin' in my bar. So, if you're gonna fight, take it outside."

The stranger threw one of his suit's gloves at Robby's feet. Picking it up, the local man shoved it in the other man's chest and stomped towards the door.

His challenger followed, trailed by many of the bar's patrons. No one wanted to miss a good fight; although, this fight wasn't that promising, seeing as how the participants were drunk.

As the spectators began clearing a space for the fight, a pretty, young redheaded woman with a security patch came over, followed by a young man who was obviously new to Mars. "Do you need some assistance," she asked with a smirk.

The Wolf turned and leered at her. "Well, darlin', we could use a ref'."

Heather glared at the man. "Well, sweetheart, you've got one," she said, her voice dripping with disgust.

Both men handed their swords to their referee. She examined them to see that both were roughly the same length. Also, she was making sure that neither sword hold a "sucker-punch" --a single shot pistol-- in the hilt. She took her duty to ensure a fair fight seriously.

Heather, satisfied that everything was even, handed the weapons back to their respecitve owners. "First blood?" she asked. Both men nodded.

She stepped back and raised her voice to address the crowd. "The rules are simple; First blood from the torso. No interference from the crowd, please, or I may be forced to ventilate some skulls." This last part brought a chuckle.

She checked her pistol, the replaced it in its holster, leaving the retention strap undone. She nodded to the combatants.

The local man, Robby, looked at his opponent. "Winner gets the loser's pistol?"

"Sounds good."

Both men nodded to Heather, then she spoke. "Begin!"

The men stumbled into their stances. Wolf chose a high stance, his broad sword hoisted above his head, ready to sweep down when his opponent made a move. Robby, on the other hand, didn't seem to have a stance. His long cavalry saber wavered slowly with the point nearly resting on the ground. The buzz of his drink was still strong in his head, and he was trying very hard to shake it off.

All of a sudden, Wolf bared his teeth and advanced on the man from Barsoom City. Apparently, this caused the adrenaline to kick in, because Robby instantly became a better swordsman. Abruptly, his sword flew up and intercepted the blade that was falling towards his head. He took a step forward and slammed his fist into the other man's stomach.

Wolf staggered back and, because he was drunk, fell onto his back, swearing and growling. The onlookers stepped back as Robby came towards the man, swinging wildly.

Ripping his scabbard off of his belt, Wolf began swinging it at Robby's knees, driving him back. Standing, he gave a savage scream and ran at Robby with his sword held high... and ended up impaled on the other man's sword.

Robby pulled his saber out of his opponent's shoulder. Stepping back, he panted, "You're pretty good... can I have my pistol now?" The "Wolf" glared at him. He unbuckled his pistol belt and dropped it on the ground, the staggered off to a doctor's office.

Griffin turned to Heather. "Whoa," he said, "that was intense."

"Happens all the time," she said, surprised at his shock.

"But... but someone could've been killed!"

"Yeah, but that rarely happens," she said. "In fact, I've never refereed a duel that went to the death."

"Mind if I ask a question?"

"Hm?"

"What's with the swords? I mean, everyone here has a sword. I understand the guns... I've even got a gun and --"

"Why aren't you wearing it," she asked.

"I dunno... should I be?"

She turned to Griffin. "Well, I don't know if you've noticed the stares, but everyone here thinks you're some sort of tourist who expects the government to defend you."

Griffin stopped and reached into his duffel bag. He pulled out a military web belt and strapped it on. Then, he reached into his bag again and came up with a holster that contained a British Webley revolver. He hooked the holster on his belt. Heather was surprised to see that it dropped almost to his knee.

"Nice rig," she said appreciatively. "Is that a real Webley?"

"No, it's a replica chambered in .44 special. But it's built from the original Webley plans."

"Very slick," she said. Then, with a grin, "Now you just need a sword. A cutlass would look really cool... with that holster so low, you already look like a swashbuckler."

"Yeah, whatever. But, again... Why do I need a sword?!?"

"A couple of reasons," she said, sounding like a teacher giving a lecture. "First (and most obvious), bullets have a bad habit of puncturing the dome. Second, swords are perhaps the best close combat weapons ever devised; and, as you can see, a lot of our disputes are settled by close combat."

This brought a chuckle from Griffin.

"Third, and most importantly, swords are the timeless symbol of the freeman. If you think about it, guns -- especially personal firearms -- are a fairly recent invention. For thousands of years before pistols and rifles, the symbol of a free man was the sword at his side. People here can be very symbolic sometimes, and they are extremely proud of being free people in the frontier. Swords are just the symbols they use to convey that."

Griffin looked thoughtful. "C'mon," she told him. "I'll walk you to Russell's office."

After a short stroll, Griffin said goodbye to Heather, and walked into Russell's Courier Service.

A man stood up from behind the office's single desk and held out his hand to the young man whose face he recognized from his personnel file. "You must be Jack," he said.

Griffin smiled and shook the offered hand. "Yes sir, but most people just call me Griffin."

"You a fan of H.G. Wells?"

"Actually, yes... The Invisible Man is one of my favorite books."

"Hm... well, did you just come to check in? According to my info, you just got here."

"Well, actually I was wondering if I could get started right away. I got plenty of rest on the ship, so I'm ready."

"Okay. Let's go." His new boss drove took Griffin to Russell Courier Service's private spaceport, giving him the history of the company as they walked.

"When I started, it was just me and a small cargo-hauler. I must've made a thousand runs between LEO and the Moon in that little thing. Anyway, I finally saved up enough to buy an interplanetary ship and that's where my business took off. Mars had just started its boom back then, and I was making a killing running supplies and construction materials. Anyway, now I've got some guys doing the cislunar run, a few ships going from Earth to Mars, and the Mars dirtside-to-orbit run, which is where you'll be flying, along with a few other pilots. Of course, if the praise from your flight instructors is any indication, you'll be doing a lot more than just orbital flights in no time."

"Well, sir, I'm not as good as all that."

"Now, don't go all modest on me, boy. I've seen your scores... you're one of the best. Here we are."

There they were indeed. Stepping through a door, they entered the main hanger space of the small spaceport. To their left was a pair of large, pressure sealed doors, which entered into the airlocks that the shuttles went through to get outdoors for launch. On the main floor sat a few atmospheric craft; gyro-planes with rotors twice as big as they would be on Earth. Next to the gyros were the shuttles, squat, bullet shaped craft with wings just barely large enough to give them stability in the thin Martian atmosphere. Dwarfing all of these, though, were a pair of enormous dirigibles. Cargo ships of the Martian deserts, the dirigibles travelled from dome to dome, carrying everything from grain to construction materials to the hemp stalks that were used to make products ranging from clothing to airship envelopes. Even some of the pistols carried by the spacers contained hemp-based polymers.

"Uh, why are the airships inflated inside," asked Griffin.

"Oh, those two are in for check-ups," replied Russell. "We've just got them filled with air to check for leaks."

He called out to a young woman who was supervising the examination of one of the ships. "Looking good, Dix?"

"Yep. She should be back in the air before tomorrow, boss!"

Russell turned to Griffin. "That's one of our dirigible pilots, Dixie," he said.

"Ah."

"And here's your ship." Russell grabbed a clipboard that hung from a ring below the cockpit. "Looks like you'll be carrying a load of mary-jane thats bound for the cancer clinic on the Moon."

Griffin smiled. "Cool," he said absent-mindedly. He was engrossed in his new ship, walking around it and brushing it with his fingertips.

"You gonna fly that thing, or ask it to dinner?"

Griffin turned, embarrassed. "Um..."

"Locker room's over there," Russell said, pointing. "There's a locker with your name on it, and a suit too."

After getting into his suit and having one of the techs check him out, Griffin walked over to his shuttle, which was already positioned inside one of the giant airlocks, and climbed up the ladder into the cockpit. A ground-crewman followed him up and, after plugging his suit into life-support, gave him a smile and a thumbs-up, then sealed him in.

He activated his comm system. "Control, this is Shuttle 76, ready for departure."

Roger, 76... hold tight. The huge door closed, and Griffin waited for the 'lock to cycle. After a few minutes, the outer door opened and a remote controlled tractor pulled him outside, where a crane lifted him onto the car of a track similar to, but shorter than, the one his shuttle had ridden on Earth.

Control made certain he was secure on the car, and he verified with his own instruments. After completing his pre-flight checks, he contacted Control. "This is Seven-Six... I'm all set."

Affirmative, Seven-Six. You are cleared for take-off. Clear skies, skipper.

Captain Jack Griffin of Mars pushed the "Execute" button on his flight computer, then leaned back and smiled.
