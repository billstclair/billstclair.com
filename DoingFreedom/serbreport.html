SLOBODAN NE! SLOBODA DA!
(No Slobodan! Yes Freedom!)

Anil Okin

The alarm goes off, every morning at 6:15! I roll around a few times, running all the reasons I should be excited to get up through my head. Then I get up. But it's not hard this morning. I don't have to think too hard, I am excited even before I open my eyes; even that exact moment when the alarm starts ringing (normally the worst part of my day) is welcome this morning.

Last night I was too tired to choose the clothes for the day. "Pants or dress? White or black? Red or purple?" Those are my usual morning questions. Not this morning. There is only one thing hanging in the closet this morning. My Serbian shirt. I bought that shirt two years ago in the Strata Sphere in Las Vegas. I saw it and homesickness overpowered me. Rebellion made me wear it during the NATO bombings. The rest of the time it sat in the closet. When I went back home I didn't even think about taking it. Why didn't I? Because wearing it would mean that I was proud of being from Serbia, and that would again mean that I was proud of Serbia. Could I be proud of Serbia? Not that Serbia, I couldn't! Not the Serbia in which people talked and thought about dignity and comfort only in the past tense. Not even in the future tense. Those things seemed long gone, and who would even dare thinking about regaining them? We all got used to our role, the role of confounded nation. The role of the martyr, the one who suffers. I saw my nation as a frog who completely forgot that she was once a beautiful princess, and that there was still a chance for a brave knight to come and break the spell with a single kiss.

But waking up this morning I knew that the spell has to be broken, that a knight was on his way. My closet was empty; only one shirt hanging in there. I wore it. Smiling. Proud. It was never a problem to walk in this shirt in the U.S. Here it was a simple revolt, but back home it was a question of pride. And I knew I could pass all the streets of Belgrade in this shirt today. And I would be proud of being a Serbian.

Every morning has become a miniature New Year's Eve for me. Opening the e-mail accumulated over the night is like unwrapping holiday gifts. I open them with anxiety to see what's in them. They tell me about love and care, whatever the actual message is about. This morning I am extra anxious. As though it were my 21st birthday. Finally I can legally drink, finally I can take all the responsibility and all the pride for what I do. Exactly like for my birthday, messages this morning come, not only from home, but from everywhere. Courtesy of our ex-president, I have friends in every corner of the world. E-mail this morning is coming from Australia, Canada, Chile, the Netherlands, Malta, the U.S., and, of course, from the epicenter of everything: Belgrade. "Good morning in freedom," "Sincere congratulations," "Cheers," they all start. Everybody's talking about excitement, and happiness, no matter how many thousands of miles away from the motherland they are.

Those writing from Belgrade are full of themselves, so important because they were actually there. So big in their own eyes. They are creating history right there, they remind me, conscious that they are entering their not-yet-born children's school textbooks. Their lives are becoming meaningful all of a sudden, and if they were to die now, they would know what they were born for. But they want to live. Now, more than ever. They are generation which wasn't given a chance. And they wanted that chance. Who can blame them now, for feeling so important when they finally won a chance to have a chance. They waited for it, for so long. They deserved it. They didn't run away. They bore with it all those years, some just not being brave enough to move, some deeply believing that there is no life for them but under that sky. They all make sure I realized I just missed the best stories for my children and grandchildren. They make sure I realize that the high from the best party we ever went to and got wasted at, times a million, wouldn't come close to what they just experienced. They make sure I realize that freedom wasn't given to them; they had to take it with their own hands.

And I realize all that... And I cry a little bit. Happy and sad at the same time. Proud of them, but envious. "I could have done the same," I tell myself. "Crazy house?" my friend is answering my rhetorical question. "Nooooooo! The revolution? Yeeeeeees!" The Revolution? A magic word, one of the rare ones I found in my textbooks that made a strong impression on me. But wasn't that supposed to be just school stuff? Everybody knows that things learned in school are not applicable to anything in real life. And how did this really happen? Beautiful pictures in front of my eyes. Hundreds of thousands of people flooded the streets. Enough energy for a nuclear reaction. Fire, flags, laughter. The television headquarters is broken into. The truth is free! The factory of lies is burning, leaving the field bare for the new seeds.

The hardest part is in front of us though, not behind. And we all know it. We have to build, not from nothingness, but we have to shovel and shovel before we get to that point. Right now, we are far below it. But we have a right to celebrate this day. It is a great one, for each one of us, no matter where we are. It's the day that gives hope.
