The WIZ Financial Model

A memory can charge a fee whenever it executes. This fee can be any amount set by the creator of the memory. For instance, I could charge a hundred dollars for a single look at a single picture that I published. Or a tenth of a cent. This is an "intellectual property" charge.

This will prove particularly important to WIZ software. For instance, every time someone hits the "center paragraph" key in some particular editor, and thus executes the <<center paragraph>> memory, its programmer gets 1/100th of a cent. This is so low no one will notice it. But if a million people center their paragraphs with it, the programmer will soon be rich. And thus, WIZ software will grow quickly as those who write it will be justly rewarded!

Or say you buy a Sears Garage Door Opening WIZ, model 123-B. Let's say the algorithm in it sucks. Someone notices that his door will open faster if he accellerates more at the start but decellerates near the end, instead of using the constant speed algorithm it came with. The person who writes a better piece of software for this particular model can charge a tiny fraction of a penny per use, and still make a forture off it.

Additionally, in the Central Memory scheme, a memory may be transmitted between two WIZes. They may also charge a transmission fee for this service. This charge could be something on the order of say one cent per megabyte. It may be different at every node - for example, high speed lines may cost more. It can be computed based on the amortization of the hardware required to effect it. It should be so low that the average person won't even notice it. Thus, a node with a very big cache would pay for itself as it would have a high percentage of cache hits and supply lots of data.

The intention in this system is to truly divide the cost of production among all those that use it. These fees should then be so low as to make cheating or bootlegging not worth the effort. If a megabyte costs a penny to transmit and use, the average WIZ will spend very little in data transmission costs. Yet if that megabyte gets used a million times, its author will make some serious profit.

Cheating will still be illegal. Thus, if you cache say, the daily New York Times, and resell the articles at a cheaper price, you are subject to severe legal penalties. Say an article costs 1 cent. A person who reads two dozen articles pays about a quarter for the paper. Now if I cache the paper and resell it, say to my friends and family, say 100 people, and charge them all the same 1 cent per article, but keep it all for myself, I make about $25. This is not worth the $10,000 fine I would get if caught. In order to make it worth the fine, I would have to resell the New York Times to tens of thousands of people. Only problem there is, once I start selling to more than my friends and family, I'm going to get caught. The chances of getting caught go up greatly with the number of people I try to sell it to. So we are back to the conclusion that, if the price is kept low enough so that INDIVIDUALS hardly notice it, and we keep it illegal with a stiff fine to cheat, then it will never be worth cheating and we will have a stable financial system.
